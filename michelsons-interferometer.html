Michelson's Interferometer

2019 Optical Design IB HL Physics Precision Measurement

Overview

Designed and constructed a Michelson interferometer from scratch for International Baccalaureate (IB) Higher Level Physics credit. The project applied the principle of light interference to make a precise optical measurement: determining the refractive index of a sample of crown glass of known thickness.

Rather than using a pre-assembled kit, all mechanical and optical components were sourced and arranged independently. A custom wooden stand was designed and manufactured to hold all components in precise alignment, which was critical for producing stable and readable interference fringes.

Principle of Operation

A Michelson interferometer works by splitting a single light beam into two paths using a beam splitter. Each path travels to a mirror and is reflected back. The two beams are then recombined, and because they have traveled different optical path lengths, they interfere constructively or destructively to produce a pattern of bright and dark fringes.

By introducing a glass sample of known thickness into one arm of the interferometer and counting the resulting fringe shifts, it is possible to calculate the optical path difference introduced by the glass. Combined with the known thickness, this gives the refractive index of the material directly from the interference data.

Mechanical Design and Construction

The wooden stand was designed to securely mount and independently adjust all optical elements: the green laser pointer source, two flat mirrors, the glass beam splitter, the crown glass sample, a focusing lens, and the digital angle measurement ruler used to set mirror angles precisely.

Alignment was the most critical challenge. Even small angular deviations in any mirror would shift the fringe pattern or collapse it entirely. The stand was designed with adjustable mounting points to allow fine-tuning of each element independently. Careful attention was paid to the rigidity of the structure to prevent vibration-induced fringe movement during measurement.

Results

The experiment successfully produced stable interference fringes and allowed for repeatable fringe-counting measurements. The calculated refractive index of the crown glass sample was consistent with the accepted literature value, validating both the optical alignment and the measurement methodology.

The project was submitted as part of the IB Higher Level Physics internal assessment and demonstrated the application of wave optics principles to a real physical measurement challenge.
